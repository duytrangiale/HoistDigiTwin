# Scenario analysis

## What this is for

A simulation only becomes a useful planning tool once you use it to answer a real question: what happens to production if something changes. This report compares three situations for the hoist circuit: a normal year, losing one skip for a week, and running the ore feed harder while the winder breaks down more often. Each was run 25 times with different random conditions, so the results reflect a typical outcome rather than one lucky or unlucky run.

## Baseline, a normal year

This is the reference point everything else is measured against: the current skip and winder setup, running under typical conditions with no unusual events. It averages 236,847 tonnes a month, which matches what earlier validation work already found for a normal month, so this is a solid starting point for comparison.

## Losing a skip for a week

The hoist circuit has two skips sharing one winder. This scenario takes one skip out of service for seven days, a realistic stand in for a breakdown or planned maintenance on that specific piece of equipment, and asks how much that costs over the year.

During the week itself, the effect is clear and expected: output drops by about 17 percent that week, and the ore storage bins fill close to their limit, since ore keeps arriving but only one skip is available to move it. That part matches common sense.

What happens next is the more useful finding. Once the second skip is back online, the system spends the following two weeks working through the backlog of ore that built up, and recovers most of the lost ground. By the end of the year, the loss is small enough that we cannot say with confidence it is a real, lasting effect rather than normal month to month variation. In plain terms: a single week without one skip is not something this operation needs to plan around at the annual level, because the other skip and the storage bins absorb most of the impact on their own.

## Running the feed harder, with more frequent breakdowns

This scenario raises how much ore is fed into the system by 30 percent, while also making the winder break down about twice as often as usual. The idea is to test a natural question: if reliability gets worse, can you make up for it by simply pushing more ore through?

The answer is no. Even with 30 percent more ore available, output still falls by close to 2 percent for the year, a loss of roughly 56,000 tonnes, and this result held up clearly across all 25 runs, not just a few unlucky ones. The reason is that the winder itself, not the ore supply, is what limits how much the operation can move. Feeding it more ore when it already cannot keep up just means more ore sits waiting, it does not create more hoisting capacity.

## The takeaway

Two practical conclusions come out of this. First, the operation has real built in resilience to a short skip outage, so a week long stoppage on one skip is not, by itself, a reason for serious concern about the year's output. Second, and more important for planning ahead, the winder is the actual limit on how much this operation can produce, not the ore feed. If the goal is to increase output or protect against a bad year, the money is better spent on winder reliability than on increasing how much ore can be fed in, since more ore alone does not help once the winder is already the constraint.
